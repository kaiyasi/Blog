## The Accidental Architect: From Code to Reality

Looking back, I feel like I accidentally stumbled onto a magnificent “ship of thieves” adventure. My days in the 10th and 11th grades seem surreal now, like a long, leisurely daydream, swinging between schoolwork and trivial club matters, never truly touching the pure core of what it means to be a “developer.” The turning point of this fate traces back to November 2025. Driven by a sudden impulse or perhaps a competitive urge to prove myself, I impulsively submitted my resume when I saw the Southern Taiwan Information Student Club United (SCIST) recruiting for their winter camp’s information team. Back then, my imagination of the “information team” was limited to backend maintenance and static webpage updates. However, fate soon gave me a grand gift—I passed the screening and unexpectedly became the lead developer for the official winter camp website.

For the next three months, my life was completely consumed by code and frameworks. I began to understand the meaning of “responsibility”—the pressure is palpable and heavy when your work is the first thing hundreds of students see. Even though the website went live on schedule, a flicker of doubt remained: as a member of the information team, my battlefield should be in the cloud, in the logic of Discord bots, or in the stable operation of servers. Was attending the physical camp really necessary? Did I need to carry heavy equipment down south for an event where I might just be an “invisible person behind the scenes”? In the end, I chose to go. I shouldered my 2.4kg Gigabyte laptop, having no idea that this machine would cause my arms to twitch uncontrollably from the long-distance weight, nor how this journey would completely shatter my definition of “winter break” and fill my second-semester schedule with various projects and activities.

On February 5, 2026, I arrived in Tainan. As a high school student living in the “Tianlong District” of Taipei for a long time, my first impression of heading south was a sense of geographical alienation. Arriving late at National Cheng Kung University (NCKU), the event had already begun. There was no reception, no guidance; I wandered alone through the unfamiliar campus with my heavy pack. Despite being staff, I felt somewhat out of place. Fortunately, several other partners who had also just arrived appeared, and I successfully checked in, receiving the walkie-talkie and staff uniform that symbolized my identity. In that moment, I truly felt lonely. Most people were friends from central and southern information clubs, chatting and laughing, while I, a “visitor from outer space” with no ties to the local info circles, could only lower my head and scroll through my phone. An uninformed onlooker would probably think I was some suspicious character wandering around.

## Trials of Fire and Silicon: Survival in Tainan

NCKU’s “Sheng-Yi Dormitory” gave me my first shock. I had read reviews before the trip, but only after visiting did I understand the meaning behind “mixed reviews.” It was a place that gave me the illusion of early military enlistment: no air conditioning, and bed boards as hard as stone. What collapsed my spirit most was discovering that the Ethernet port in my room was completely non-functional that first night. In that sweltering, enclosed room, having no internet was akin to oxygen deprivation for an IT person. At the time, my heart was full of complaints about southern public facilities, only to realize after returning to Taipei that it was my own Ethernet cable that had long since broken internally—the four-day “no-internet anxiety” was a massive misunderstanding. And the mosquitoes in Tainan were clearly influenced by the city’s sweet-leaning culinary culture, having consumed far too much sugar. They were large and flew slowly; though easy to catch, they moved in “legions.” In that density, you’d even have the illusion that just by waving your hands, you could catch a few in mid-air. If I hadn’t been in a campus dorm, I would have really wanted to light some mosquito coils and fight a decisive battle with these “sugar warriors.”

On the first day, the courses focused on cybersecurity and computer science theoretical frameworks. The general coordinator arranged for me to support the curriculum team as a teaching assistant, but due to the lack of hands-on segments, I looked like a lost passerby all day, still lugging that heavy laptop. However, on the second day, the atmosphere changed abruptly. With lectures from industry giants like Systex, DEVCORE, and CyCraft, I finally entered the zone. Listening to seniors share first-hand experiences of cybersecurity defense and the chess matches against hackers in the workplace, my “engineer soul” was thoroughly ignited. That thirst for technology even made me silently wish: if given the chance in the future, I must work at Systex to experience that top-tier technical atmosphere. In the midst of the chaotic night, the only thing that could soothe the soul was Tainan’s unique eel noodles. Late that night, I ordered takeout with the students. The crisp, springy eel paired with perfectly sweet and sour noodles made all the hard work seem to vanish with the food. Although I accidentally dropped an 80 TWD milk tea while helping, causing me to lose a “huge sum,” the junior’s thoughtful “Senior, let me pay for you” gave me a sense of warmth.

The third day was “hell time” for the curriculum team. The courses introduced AI implementation and complex technical layers, and students’ environment issues sprouted like mushrooms after rain. The design of NCKU’s Qi-Duan Hall was extremely unfriendly to TAs—the awkward steps and narrow aisles had us running until our backs ached. We had to digest various “psychic-level” problems in a short time, from broken Python environments to invalid API keys. The most chaotic moment was when hundreds of students registered for a free AI platform’s tokens simultaneously, causing NCKU’s local network to be flagged and blocked as a malicious attack. At that moment, the entire venue fell into technical paralysis; it was a real technical disaster and the best stage to test the curriculum team’s spontaneous reaction. During these four days, fire alarms seemed to become NCKU’s scheduled background music. While corporate lectures were at their most profound, the alarm blared, instantly waking everyone up. Later we learned it was a false alarm from the electrical engineering building lab. I thought this was just an episode, but at 2 AM, the dormitory fire alarm rang again. Sleepily inquiring, the answer I got was “fire drill”—a truly bittersweet response.

## Lingering Ripples: A New Horizon at the End of the Track

The hackathon presentation on the final day was my most shocking moment. In just four days, these students went from zero to developing fully functional Discord bots, speech-to-text systems, and even a Minecraft plugin that allowed players to execute complex in-game commands via AI chat. Looking at these products, I silently took photos—these creative ideas and implementation skills are all nutrients for my future development. However, the end of the event was still full of “extreme challenges.” Because I had to catch the High-Speed Rail back to Taipei, I had to skip the final celebration dinner. On the way back, because I miscalculated the shuttle time, I almost missed the last train. Fortunately, I met a highly skilled taxi driver who nimbly maneuvered through small alleys, winning me precious time. I rushed into the HSR station at the 26th minute, and the train departed at the 28th. In that moment, watching the southern scenery retreat rapidly outside the window, my heart held not just the palpitations of a close call, but a surreal sense of fulfillment.

The “ripples” this event brought me were much larger than I expected. After returning to Taipei, I didn’t return to my originally plain life. Instead, I joined one “tech giant” group after another. Those developers who used to exist only in legends are now people I can exchange ideas with anytime. I opened the door to a new world, met more “tech gods,” and unexpectedly, as if guided by fate, took on the position of SCAICT Information Team Lead. All these chain reactions seem to be paving the way for my future studies at National Chung Hsing University (NCHU) and my future career development. Most coincidentally, during the camp, I actually met a senior from the same department at NCHU. The world is incredibly small, as if all efforts and decisions eventually converge at some intersection.

This four-day, three-night trip to NCKU was not just a winter camp; it was a springboard that pushed me from a simple code-writer into a broader community and field of practice. Although the process involved backaches, mosquito bites, fire alarm scares, and the regret of a spilled milk tea, the experience of engaging with these “tech gods” and overcoming difficulties with partners is something no textbook can provide. I am glad I boarded this “ship of thieves.” Although my future schedule is already so full it’s a bit suffocating, I know this is what growth looks like. I look forward to the next encounter, to continue writing my technical chapters among this group of passionate information enthusiasts.
